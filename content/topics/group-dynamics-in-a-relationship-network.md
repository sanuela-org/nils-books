# Group Dynamics in a Relationship Network

## Definition

A relationship network is not a couple with extra people bolted on. It is a group with its own emergent dynamics. When three or more people share closeness, the system develops properties no single pair contains: tensions travel, trust accumulates across the circle, and everyone's wellbeing affects everyone else. Treating the network as a living group — rather than a collection of private dyads — is what keeps it stable. The book [[books/wide-open/wide-open|*Wide Open*]] describes it like this: each connection contributes to the health of the whole garden.

## Conflicts send ripples

In a closed couple, a fight stays between two people. In a network, every conflict ripples outward. When two members argue or drift apart, the others feel it — in group energy, in cancelled plans, in the unspoken tension that fills a shared room.

- A disagreement about scheduling does not only affect the two people directly involved. It reshapes everyone's expectations.
- Resentment that goes unspoken between two members quietly erodes trust across the whole circle.
- Silence after a fight can feel like a shared punishment, even for those who were not part of the conflict.
- The ripple effect means that ignoring problems between two people is never truly a private matter.

## Triangulation versus healthy mediation

When tension arises, people often pull in a third party to take their side. This is triangulation — and it damages the network. It converts a problem between two people into a coalition against one, breeding mistrust and factionalism.

- Triangulation uses a third person as a sounding board, an ally, or a messenger. The original conflict is never addressed directly between the two who own it.
- Healthy mediation is different. A third party holds space neutrally, helping both sides communicate without choosing sides. Their role is to stabilise, not to validate one perspective over another.
- Knowing when to seek a balanced third perspective — and when to speak directly with the person involved — is one of the hardest skills in network life.

## Shared carrying

The network model distributes emotional weight. No single partner is expected to meet every need. When one person is struggling, the others can hold space. This takes pressure off any one connection and creates genuine resilience.

- Each person in the circle shares responsibility for the group's emotional climate, not only their own.
- Carrying a burden together means nobody is left alone with pain, fear, or insecurity.
- The network does not eliminate individual accountability. It adds collective care on top of it.
- When everyone feels responsible for the whole, the web becomes self-sustaining rather than fragile.

## Group-level awareness

Most people learn to think about relationships one pair at a time. They focus on their own bond and treat the rest as background. Group-level awareness means stepping back and seeing the full web — how bonds influence each other, how one person's joy or fear moves through the circle.

- Pairwise thinking asks: *How are we doing?* Group awareness asks: *How are we all doing?*
- Seeing the system helps people notice when one relationship is over-functioning and another is being neglected.
- The network is more than the sum of its dyads. Its health depends on attention to the spaces between all the connections.
- Regular group check-ins, shared rituals, and open conversation about how the network is evolving keep the web transparent and alive.
